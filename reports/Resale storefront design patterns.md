# Plain browsing and honest disclosure sell secondhand clothing

The strongest-converting resale and small fashion storefronts don't win on visual spectacle. They make browsing easy and tell shoppers up front what they need to know. They use a static hero and skip rotating carousels. They put categories in plain view, with size as a first-class way to shop. Every product card shows the same few facts: price, size and condition. They state shipping, returns and condition terms before checkout, and their urgency comes from facts that are actually true of one-of-one stock. The usability research points the same way as the stores. Baymard's 2024 apparel testing found that **100% of participants relied mainly on navigation and browsing and only 10% used search**. In another Baymard test, **39% of shoppers found out on the product page that the item wasn't in their size**. That problem is worse for a resale store, where every item exists in exactly one size. For Velocity Shop, which uses Square checkout, runs as a static site and has no on-site reviews, the evidence supports eight patterns, set out in the table in the fourth section. All of them are cheap to build on a static site. Two caveats about the evidence. First, every source in the underlying notes came from search snippets and secondary write-ups, because live store pages and Baymard's and NN/g's primary pages could not be fetched. Hero copy, card hover behaviour and footer contents of named stores are therefore unverified. Second, no public controlled study tests resale-specific homepage layouts. The recommendations combine general ecommerce usability research with patterns that recur across resale stores.

## Browsing structure, not the hero, carries the homepage

The research on above-the-fold design mostly tells stores what to avoid. Baymard found that **52% of mobile sites auto-rotate their homepage carousel**. In testing, auto-rotation sent participants on detours they didn't intend, and Baymard reports that showing the same content as static sections on a taller page performs just as well and is much simpler to build ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/homepage-carousel)). An older NN/g test makes the same point: a user failed to find a target set in 98-point type because the carousel moved on before they got to it. Promo-styled heroes also run into banner blindness ([Yale Usability](https://usability.yale.edu/usability-best-practices/hero-imagescarousels)). The homepage's real job is to show the range. Baymard's guideline #237 found that shoppers misread a store's scope when the homepage showed too few product types, and it recommends featuring **at least 40% of product types** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-ux-best-practices)). The admired small brands fit this pattern. Aimé Leon Dore pairs one full-bleed film photograph with a near-monochrome interface ([shadcn.io](https://www.shadcn.io/design/aime-leon-dore)). Online Ceramics' stripped-back site expresses its DIY ethos ([Grow Your Clothing Brand](https://growyourclothingbrand.com/blog/case-studies/online-ceramics/)). In both cases the distinctive look comes from one strong choice of art direction, not from stacked effects. Ader Error's autoplaying video with sound is the cautionary example ([Webflow](https://webflow.com/blog/10-brutalist-websites)).

Navigation is where resale stores actually set themselves apart. NN/g's 2016 study (179 participants) found that navigation hidden behind a menu **cut discoverability by about 20%** compared with visible navigation. Tasks took 39% longer on desktop and 15% longer on mobile ([Smart Insights summary](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/are-hamburger-menus-hidden-navigation-good-for-ux/)). Baymard found that **33% of mobile sites don't make product categories the top-level menu items**. When categories were nested under a "Shop" link, users gave up on the menu and switched to search ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/main-navigation-product-categories)). Since browsing is how almost all apparel shoppers find products ([Baymard apparel](https://baymard.com/blog/apparel-search)), the category structure matters more than the hero. The praised resale and vintage sites add their own axes to it. Classic Football Shirts puts an "Eras" block of decade links on its homepage next to a "Product Styles" block, and its shop menu separates Original Vintage, Match-Worn and Reissues ([classicfootballshirts.com](https://www.classicfootballshirts.com/)). Ragstock lets shoppers browse vintage by decade ([Pebble Mag](https://pebblemag.com/vintage-clothing-online/)). Beyond Retro mixes "Best of New In", "Workwear" and decade collections ([shop.beyondretro.com](https://shop.beyondretro.com/)). ThredUp, a general thrift store, leads with department plus "Top Brands" and puts its sell-side "Clean Out" service at the top level ([ThredUp](https://www.thredup.com/bg/category/brands)).

Size is the most important "shop by" option. When Baymard put the size filter at the top of the filter list and expanded it by default, **90% of participants filtered by size first** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/apparel-put-size-filter-near-top-and-expand-for-sidebar-filtering)). On mobile, a sticky filter/sort bar on collection pages lifted conversion **21.39%** in one Shopify agency A/B test. That is a single vendor case study, but it fits Baymard's finding that mobile shoppers wear themselves out going back and forth between the product list and the filter screen ([Blend Commerce](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/ab-tests-shopify/sticky-filter-and-sort-on-mobile-collection-pages-boosts-ecommerce-revenue); [Baymard](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/filtering-options)). One tension in the evidence: most benchmarked mobile sites still hide navigation behind a hamburger ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/navigation-menu)). That is the platform default, not a finding that it works. A small catalog can fit all its categories in a scrollable chip row, so there's no reason to hide them.

## Product cards must answer "what, what size, what condition, how much"

Baymard's product-list research covered 19 sites and produced 83 guidelines. It found that **64% of sites present product-list information inadequately**. When cards lacked key attributes, participants bounced between the list and product pages, and that often ended in abandonment ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/product-listing-information)). The fix is to show the same attributes in the same place on every card, with "just enough" to judge whether an item is relevant. Resale marketplaces agree on what those attributes are. Depop, Vinted, Grailed, Poshmark and Vestiaire all treat **brand, size and condition** as standard fields on every listing ([Depop fields via Apify](https://apify.com/markdolanmoore/depop-scraper); [Grailed fields via Apify](https://apify.com/hipersoft/grailed-scraper); [desirabilitylab.com](https://desirabilitylab.com/backfill/backfill-2024-21-depop-vs-grailed-vs-poshmark)). The one card element confirmed across several large resellers is a **current price shown against a reference price**. ThredUp strikes through a figure it explicitly calls an *estimate* of what a comparable new item would cost, and its marketing leads with "up to 90% off est. retail" ([ThredUp product page](https://www.thredup.com/product/women-cotton-madewell-white-pullover-sweater/225639486); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.thredup.android&hl=en_US)). Poshmark shows an optional crossed-out "original price" that sellers may estimate ([Poshmark](https://support.poshmark.com/s/article/894455911?language=en_US)). A small seller should copy this only with an honest label such as "est. retail", or leave it out.

Images and pagination matter too. A Baymard guideline heading recommends **three or more thumbnails per item in product lists, noting that 80% of sites don't offer them** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/collections/product-list)). For one-off stock, a second image of the back, label or a flaw gives real information. Hover-swap images, however, are untested, and on touch screens the first tap can be used up revealing the second image. Baymard's study of more than 50 sites found that **"Load more" with lazy-loading beats both pagination and infinite scroll**, which it calls "downright harmful" on mobile ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2016/03/pagination-infinite-scrolling-load-more-buttons/)). Titles longer than about 30–40 characters get truncated in mobile grids ([GrowthSuite](https://www.growthsuite.net/questions/are-my-product-titles-truncating-in-collection-grids-and-confusing-visitors)). That favours a short title with the important facts first, such as era, brand and item, the formula reseller copy guides recommend ([Vintage Labels UK](https://thevintagelabels.com/blog/where-to-sell-vintage-clothing-uk/)). There is no evidence for a particular grid density. Two columns on mobile is industry convention, not a research finding. Yeezy Supply's grid without titles or prices ([Webflow](https://webflow.com/blog/10-brutalist-websites)) is distinctive but wrong for resale, where size and price decide the purchase. Even brutalist ecommerce guides keep price, size, stock and the CTA as the highest-contrast elements on the page ([superdesign.dev](https://superdesign.dev/styles/brutalism/ecommerce)).

## Trust comes from disclosure, not badges

The abandonment numbers show where trust breaks down. In Baymard's 2025 survey, **39% of shoppers who abandoned a purchase blamed extra costs, 19% didn't trust the site with their card, 15% disliked the returns policy and 14% couldn't see the total cost up front** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment)). **64% of users look for shipping costs on the product page before deciding to buy** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages)). NN/g's trust framework names "up-front disclosure" as one of its four pillars ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). For a store that hands off to Square, this matters a lot. If shipping cost first appears inside Square's checkout, the shopper meets it at the worst possible moment. Generous returns are not required. ThredUp openly doesn't refund original shipping and makes final-sale items non-returnable ([ThredUp help](https://help.thredup.com/en_us/what-is-thredup-s-return-policy-HJjbVX1jn.md)). What matters is that limits are stated before the purchase.

Condition is the trust signal specific to resale. In February 2025 eBay replaced generic "Pre-owned" clothing listings with **three grades: Excellent, Good and Fair**. The Good grade requires every imperfection to be shown and described ([eBay.ca](https://community.ebay.ca/t5/Announcements/Introducing-new-conditions-for-pre-loved-clothing/ba-p/515845)). Patagonia Worn Wear defines its three grades (Excellent, Great, Good) on every product page and adds notes for each item ([Worn Wear](https://wornwear.patagonia.com/products/half-mass_48274_bch)). The RealReal runs five tiers from Pristine to Fair, plus an "As Is" tier ([TRR RealStyle](https://realstyle.therealreal.com/condition-in-luxury-resale/)). The common failure is grade inflation. Vestiaire forum buyers report "very good" items arriving more worn than expected ([PurseForum](https://forum.purseblog.com/threads/vestiaire-collective-experiences.770874/page-91)). Independent vintage sellers back up grades with measurements. They give flat pit-to-pit, length and sleeve figures labelled "laid flat", because tag sizes from past decades are unreliable ([Depop measuring guide](https://depopautomation.com/blog/how-to-measure-clothes-for-depop-listings)). The big platforms build trust by publishing their process. Worn Wear says each item is "inspected, authenticated, graded for condition and repaired when needed" ([Worn Wear](https://wornwear.patagonia.com/)). A small seller can't honestly claim authentication, but it can truthfully say it inspects, measures and photographs each actual item.

Velocity Shop has no on-site reviews, and that gap is real. Spiegel Research Center found purchase likelihood **270% higher for a product with five reviews than for one with none**. The lift was largest for higher-priced items, and purchase likelihood peaked at 4.0–4.7 stars rather than a perfect 5.0 ([Spiegel](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales)). The legal limits are also firm. The FTC's rule in force since October 2024 bans fake or AI-written reviews, review suppression and fake social-influence metrics, with civil penalties of about **$51,744 per violation** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). Fashion Nova paid **$4.2M** over allegedly hiding reviews below four stars ([Olshan Law](https://www.olshanlaw.com/Advertising-Law-Blog/alert-to-ecommerce-marketers-suppressing-negative-reviews-can-lead)). The workable option is to borrow verifiable reputation from elsewhere, such as an eBay feedback profile, a Google Business rating or an Instagram account. NN/g lists "connection to the rest of the web" as a trust factor ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)), but no study measures how much off-site review links lift conversion.

Payment badges are weaker evidence than vendors claim. Baymard found that perceived security comes mostly from how secure a page *looks*, and that even a generic seal the researchers made up raised perceived security ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/site-seal-trust)). That is a reason to be cautious, not permission to use a fake seal. In CXL's eye-tracking study, recognisable names (PayPal, Google, Norton) were the ones remembered as adding security ([CXL](https://conversionxl.com/research-study/checkout-optimization/)). The published conversion lifts from badges come from vendor tests that bundled several changes together ([VWO](https://vwo.com/blog/32-increase-in-conversions-by-ab-testing-for-the-right-reasons)). Square says its hosted checkout is PCI-DSS compliant and that card data is encrypted so it never touches the seller ([Square](https://squareup.com/gb/en/payments/secure)). A factual line naming Square, placed next to every outbound buy link, is therefore honest and uses a recognisable brand.

## Eight patterns Velocity Shop should adopt

The table turns the evidence into a build list ordered by expected impact. "Evidence" separates tested usability or field data from patterns observed at stores.

Some of the evidence behind patterns 6 and 8 deserves more detail. Speed effects are large but vary by site. Farfetch, a fashion retailer, saw **conversion fall about 1.3% for each extra 100 ms of LCP** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/farfetch/)). Vodafone's 50/50 test produced **8% more sales from a 31% better LCP** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/vodafone)). A static site already has most of the advantage; the risk is oversized product photos and images without set dimensions. Accessibility failures are concentrated where clothing grids are weak. The 2025 WebAIM Million found **low-contrast text on 79.1% of home pages and missing alt text on 55.5%** ([WebAIM](https://webaim.org/projects/million/2025)). In the UK Click-Away Pound survey, **69% of disabled shoppers said they would leave a hard-to-use site** and only 8% would report the problem ([Click-Away Pound](https://www.clickawaypound.com/downloads/CAP2019PR2.pdf)). On drops, the evidence covers two approaches. One is fixed-cadence calm: Noah releases on Thursdays, and ALD lets pieces sit on the site for weeks ([Desirability Lab](https://desirabilitylab.com/backfill/backfill-2025-15-aim-leon-dore-styling)). The other is countdown-and-password theatre, which off-the-shelf "drop" themes have turned into a template cliché ([getdroptheme.com](https://getdroptheme.com/)). One-of-one stock fits the calm model, because its scarcity is real and needs no staging.

One open question remains: what to do with sold items. The Shopify default is to unpublish them the moment they sell ([commercerank.ai](https://www.commercerank.ai/guides/themes/best-vintage-themes)). Merchants selling one-off items ask for "recently sold" sections, though ([Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/setup-for-a-recently-sold-unique-products-collection/282593)). The one source arguing that hiding sold items costs search traffic sells a sorting app ([Dynasort](https://dynasort.io/sold-out-products-showing-first-shopify/)). No study compares the two approaches. A middle path keeps a separate "Recently sold" strip or archive page as real proof that stock moves, and keeps sold items out of the main grid so shoppers don't hit dead ends. It only works if the sold state stays in sync with Square.

The footer has the weakest evidence of any area. Confirmed elements are shipping and returns policies ([RJP Design](https://rjp.design/vintage-store-website)), an about or story page (Beyond Retro's "Our Vintage", [Beyond Retro](https://www.beyondretro.com/pages/about-us)), sell or trade-in links where they apply (ThredUp Clean Out, [Patagonia Trade-In](https://www.patagonia.com/trade-in/)) and an email signup. Sources disagree on whether footer signups convert. SplitBase says few people look at the footer ([SplitBase](https://splitbase.com/blog/luxury-ecommerce-crm)), while Inbox Collective calls it a high-intent spot ([Inbox Collective](https://inboxcollective.com/owned-growth-footer/)). Both agree on an email-only field with a concrete stated benefit, so "Get Friday drop alerts" beats "Newsletter". An Instagram strip in the footer helps the brand but does little for direct conversion ([Curator.io](https://curator.io/blog/website-footer-examples)). A sensible Velocity Shop footer holds shop links, a help column (Shipping, Returns, Condition guide, Measurements, Contact), About, the drop-alert signup, social and reputation links, and the Square secure-checkout line.

## Conclusion

The research changes where a small resale store's design effort should go. A small store competes with ThredUp and Depop mainly on clarity, not on the hero. Shoppers get to the right item by browsing, and they decide based on size, condition and total cost. Shoppers are already wary of secondhand quality and of leaving the site for payment, and a small store can address both by stating facts that are true of its stock. "1 of 1", a defined condition grade, a laid-flat measurement, a flat shipping rate and a named payment processor all do this, and none of them can be faked without legal risk. That makes Velocity Shop's apparent handicaps (no reviews, an off-site checkout, no app) cheaper to overcome than they look. Design effort is better spent on a consistent photo setup and a well-structured category, era and size index than on visual effects.

Two things are still unknown and worth testing on the live site. No study measures whether off-site reputation links make up for missing on-site reviews, or whether a visible sold archive lifts or lowers conversion for one-of-one stock. Square analytics on click-throughs from cards with and without a "Recently sold" strip, and with and without an eBay feedback link, would answer questions that no published research has.
